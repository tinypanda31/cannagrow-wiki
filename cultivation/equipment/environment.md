# Cannabis Environmental Control Equipment

The grow environment determines everything — vapor pressure deficit (VPD), disease pressure, terpene expression, and overall plant vigor. You can have perfect genetics, premium nutrients, and ideal light spectrum, but if your environment is wrong, none of it matters. This guide covers every piece of hardware you need to dial in temperature, humidity, and airflow in a cannabis grow space.

Getting this right means the difference between dense, resinous flowers and a crop destroyed by powdery mildew, botrytis, or heat stress. Every section below includes practical calculations, worked examples, and tier-based buying guidance so you can make informed decisions regardless of your budget.

## Calculating Your Ventilation Needs

Proper ventilation is the foundation of environmental control. The goal is simple: remove hot, stale air and replace it with fresh air at a rate that keeps temperature and humidity in your target ranges.

### CFM Calculation Formula

The starting point for selecting an inline fan is calculating the minimum cubic feet per minute (CFM) your grow space needs:

**Basic formula:**

```
Room Volume (ft³) = Length × Width × Height
Minimum CFM = Room Volume × Air Changes per Hour (ACH) ÷ 60
```

**Air changes per hour** for cannabis typically range from 30 to 60, depending on the heat load:

| Condition | Air Changes per Hour |
|---|---|
| LED lights, mild climate, cool tent room | 20–30 |
| LED lights, warm climate | 30–40 |
| HPS/MH lights, standard tent | 40–50 |
| HPS lights, hot climate | 50–60+ |

**Adjustments** — multiply your base CFM by each factor that applies:

| Factor | Multiplier |
|---|---|
| Carbon filter attached | × 1.25 (25% resistance) |
| Each 90° duct bend | × 1.10 |
| Long duct run (>15 ft) | × 1.15 |
| Very hot climate (+15°F above target) | × 1.20 |
| Ballast inside the tent | × 1.10 |

**Final CFM = Base CFM × all applicable multipliers**

### Worked Example: 4×4×6.5 ft Tent with LED

Let's walk through a real calculation:

1. **Room volume:** 4 × 4 × 6.5 = **104 ft³**
2. **Base CFM:** 104 × 30 ACH ÷ 60 = **52 CFM**
3. **Apply adjustments:**
   - Carbon filter: 52 × 1.25 = 65
   - One 90° bend: 65 × 1.10 = 71.5
   - Short duct run (under 10 ft): no adjustment
4. **Final required CFM: ~72 CFM minimum**

In practice, you would round up and select a fan rated for **100–150 CFM** at variable speed. This gives you headroom for hotter days and lets you run the fan at 50–70% speed for quieter operation. Running a larger fan at lower speed is always better than running a small fan at 100%.

### Worked Example: 8×8×8 ft Room with HPS

1. **Room volume:** 8 × 8 × 8 = **512 ft³**
2. **Base CFM:** 512 × 50 ACH ÷ 60 = **427 CFM**
3. **Apply adjustments:**
   - Carbon filter: 427 × 1.25 = 534
   - Two 90° bends: 534 × 1.10 × 1.10 = 646
   - Long duct run: 646 × 1.15 = 743
   - Ballast in tent: 743 × 1.10 = 817
4. **Final required CFM: ~820 CFM**

For this setup, you would want a fan rated for **1,000–1,200 CFM** to ensure adequate capacity at the operating speed you choose.

### CFM Quick Reference Table

| Grow Space | Volume (ft³) | Base CFM (30 ACH) | Recommended Fan CFM (with filter + bends) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2×4×6 ft tent | 48 | 24 | 60–100 |
| 3×3×6.5 ft tent | 59 | 30 | 75–125 |
| 4×4×6.5 ft tent | 104 | 52 | 100–200 |
| 4×8×6.5 ft tent | 208 | 104 | 200–350 |
| 5×5×6.5 ft tent | 163 | 82 | 175–300 |
| 6×6×8 ft room | 288 | 144 | 300–500 |
| 8×8×8 ft room | 512 | 256 | 500–900 |
| 10×10×8 ft room | 800 | 400 | 800–1,200 |

> **Rule of thumb:** Always buy a fan rated for at least 1.5–2× your calculated minimum CFM. This gives you control headroom, accounts for filter degradation over time, and lets you run quieter at lower speeds.

## Inline Fans (Exhaust)

The inline fan is the lung of your grow operation. It pulls air through your carbon filter, removes heat and humidity, and creates the negative pressure that keeps odors contained.

### Types of Inline Fans

| Type | Description | Best For |
|---|---|---|
| **Axial** | Blade-style, moves high volume at low static pressure. Cheapest option. | Short duct runs, minimal resistance |
| **Mixed-flow** | Impeller design, handles static pressure well. Quieter and more efficient. | Most grow tent setups with carbon filters |
| **EC motor** | Electronically commutated, highly efficient, very quiet, precise speed control. Long lifespan. | Serious growers, noise-sensitive locations |
| **AC motor** | Traditional motor, less efficient, runs hotter, less precise speed control. | Budget setups |

**Axial fans** lose significant performance when connected to carbon filters and ducting because their design is optimized for moving free air with minimal resistance. For any setup with a carbon filter (which is most indoor grows), a mixed-flow or centrifugal fan is the better choice.

**EC motors** are worth the premium if you can afford them. They use 40–60% less electricity, produce significantly less noise and heat, and often last 5–10 years longer than AC motors. The energy savings can pay back the price difference within a year or two of continuous operation.

### What to Look For

- **Variable speed control** — essential. You will rarely run at 100%. A PWM or 0–10V controller lets you dial in the exact airflow.
- **Duct size** — match your ducting diameter. Common sizes: 4", 6", 8". Larger ducts move more air with less noise.
- **Noise rating** — measured in decibels (dB). Under 36 dB is very quiet. Over 48 dB is loud. Mixed-flow designs are generally quieter than axial.
- **Max static pressure rating** — important if you have long duct runs or multiple bends. Centrifugal fans handle static pressure better.
- **IP rating** — if your grow space gets humid, look for at least IP44 to protect against moisture ingress.
- **Warranty** — reputable brands offer 2–5 year warranties. Budget brands may offer none.

### Inline Fan Brand Tiers

| Tier | Price Range (4–6") | Brands | What to Expect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Budget** | $25–$60 | Vivosun, iPower, AC Infinity Cloudline (entry) | Adequate for small tents, louder, shorter lifespan, basic AC motors, may vibrate at high speed. Good for starting out. |
| **Mid** | $60–$150 | AC Infinity Cloudline T-Series, Hurricane, Prima Klima | Mixed-flow design, variable speed, reasonable noise levels, 2–3 year warranty. The sweet spot for most hobby growers. |
| **Premium** | $150–$400+ | Systemair RVK, Soler & Palau, AC Infinity Cloudline PRO | EC motors, extremely quiet (25–32 dB), PWM control, 5+ year warranty, built-in environmental sensors, app connectivity. Built to run continuously for years. |

### Fan Placement

The inline fan should be placed **after** the carbon filter (pulling air through the filter) rather than before it (pushing air through). Pulling is more efficient because:

1. The fan operates in cleaner, filtered air, extending its lifespan.
2. Negative pressure inside the filter ensures all air passes through the carbon bed — no odor bypass.
3. Most fan manufacturers rate their CFM for pull (suction) configurations.

Mount the fan as high as practical in the tent (heat rises) and keep the duct run as short and straight as possible. Every bend and foot of duct reduces effective CFM.

## Carbon Filters

Carbon filters are the primary odor control method for indoor cannabis cultivation. Activated carbon adsorbs volatile organic compounds (VOCs) — the terpenes that give flowering cannabis its distinctive smell — preventing them from escaping your grow space.

### How They Work

Air is drawn through a bed of activated carbon. The carbon has an enormous surface area (500–1,500 m² per gram) with microscopic pores that trap odor molecules through adsorption. The **bed depth** (thickness of the carbon layer) is the single most important specification:

| Bed Depth | Effectiveness | Lifespan |
|---|---|---|
| 10–15 mm | Poor — odor breakthrough in weeks | 1–2 months |
| 20–30 mm | Adequate for light odor | 2–4 months |
| 40–50+ mm | Good — standard quality | 6–12 months |
| 60+ mm | Excellent — premium filters | 12–18 months |

**Thicker carbon beds** last longer and handle higher airflow without odor breakthrough. Cheap filters with thin beds may seem like a bargain but need replacing far more often, making them more expensive in the long run.

### Sizing Your Carbon Filter

The filter's CFM rating must **match or exceed** your fan's maximum CFM. If your fan moves more air than the filter is rated for, air will pass through too quickly for adequate odor removal.

**Rule: Filter CFM rating ≥ Fan max CFM**

### Carbon Filter Sizing Table

| Grow Space | Filter Diameter | Minimum CFM Rating | Recommended CFM Rating |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2×4 ft tent | 4" | 50 | 75–100 |
| 3×3 ft tent | 4" | 60 | 100–125 |
| 4×4 ft tent | 6" | 100 | 150–200 |
| 4×8 ft tent | 6" | 200 | 250–350 |
| 5×5 ft tent | 6" | 150 | 200–300 |
| 6×6 ft room | 8" | 300 | 400–500 |
| 8×8 ft room | 8" | 500 | 600–800 |
| 10×10 ft room | 8" | 800 | 1,000–1,200 |

> **Diameter rule:** As your grow space gets larger, increase the filter diameter, not just the length. A 6" filter handles significantly more airflow than a 4" filter with less resistance. For spaces over 25 ft², go to 6". For spaces over 100 ft², go to 8".

### Carbon Filter Quality Checklist

When evaluating a carbon filter, check:

- **Carbon weight** — more activated carbon (by weight) generally means longer life and better odor control. A quality 6" filter typically contains 10-20 lbs of activated carbon; premium 8" filters may contain 25+ lbs. Australian-sourced virgin coal-based carbon (RC412/RX412) is widely considered the gold standard.
- **Carbon source** — Australian-sourced virgin coal-based carbon is widely regarded as the gold standard. Avoid filters that use recycled or reactivated carbon.
- **Flange** — a sturdy, well-sealed flange prevents air bypass. Look for thick rubber gaskets and solid metal construction.
- **Pre-filter sleeve** — a washable outer pre-filter extends the life of the carbon by catching dust, trichomes, and debris before they clog the carbon bed. Replace pre-filters every 3–6 months.
- **Mesh screen** — the inner mesh should be fine enough to prevent carbon dust from escaping but open enough to not restrict airflow.

### Pre-Filter Maintenance

- **Inspect monthly** — shake off loose debris and check for clogging.
- **Vacuum** — use a shop vac on low suction to clean the pre-filter surface every 4–6 weeks.
- **Replace** — pre-filter sleeves should be replaced every 3–6 months depending on how dusty your environment is.
- **Carbon filter lifespan** — even well-maintained carbon filters lose effectiveness over time. Replace the entire filter every 12–18 months, or sooner if you notice any odor escaping.

## Oscillating Fans (Canopy Airflow)

While the inline fan handles air exchange with the outside, oscillating fans manage the air movement **inside** the grow space. This internal airflow is just as critical and is often overlooked by beginners.

### Why Internal Airflow Matters

- **Prevents microclimates** — stagnant pockets of humid air around dense colas are where botrytis (bud rot) starts. Moving air eliminates these pockets.
- **Strengthens stems** — gentle, consistent airflow creates micro-stress on stems, causing plants to develop thicker, stronger branches that support heavier flowers.
- **Improves gas exchange** — moving air at the leaf surface ensures a steady supply of CO₂ for photosynthesis and removes the oxygen boundary layer that can slow photosynthesis.
- **Reduces disease** — powdery mildew, botrytis, and damping-off all thrive in stagnant conditions. Good airflow is the first line of defense.
- **Aids transpiration** — moving air increases the transpiration rate, which drives nutrient uptake from the roots.

### Types of Internal Fans

| Type | Pros | Cons | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clip-on fans** | Mount anywhere, cheap, good airflow direction | Limited coverage area, clips can fail | Small tents, targeted spot cooling |
| **Pedestal fans** | Adjustable height, wide oscillation, powerful | Takes floor space, can be knocked over | Medium to large tents, rooms |
| **Wall-mount fans** | Out of the way, permanent, stable | Requires mounting, less flexible | Permanent grow rooms |
| **Inline circulation fans** | Designed for grow spaces, gentle wide pattern | More expensive, specialized | Serious setups with multiple canopy levels |

### Fan Placement Strategy

The goal is to create **gentle, chaotic air movement** throughout the entire canopy — not a direct, concentrated blast on any single plant.

**General placement guidelines:**

- **Position fans** to blow across the canopy, not directly down at it. Aim for air to circulate **through** the plants, not just over them.
- **Use at least two fans** in any space larger than 3×3 ft. Place them on opposite sides, angled so their airflow patterns intersect and create circulation rather than a single-direction wind tunnel.
- **Oscillation is preferred** — the moving pattern prevents any one area from getting constant direct wind, which can cause wind burn (crispy, curled leaf edges).
- **One fan below the canopy** — if your plants are bushy and dense, position a small fan to move air through the lower canopy and stem area where humidity accumulates.
- **Speed setting** — on low to medium. You want leaves to gently sway, not thrash. If leaves are constantly bent backward, the fan is too strong or too close.

### Oscillating vs Fixed Fans

| | Oscillating | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Coverage | Wide area, moving pattern | Targeted, single direction |
| Wind burn risk | Low | Higher if aimed directly at plants |
| Cost | Slightly more | Slightly less |
| Best use | General canopy circulation | Spot cooling hot areas, drying harvested buds |

For most grows, oscillating fans are the better choice. Fixed fans are useful for addressing specific hot spots or for the drying room.

## Humidifiers

Humidifiers add moisture to the air. They are essential during the seedling and clone stages (when 65–75% RH is ideal) and useful in dry climates or winter grows when indoor air becomes extremely dry.

### When You Need a Humidifier

- **Seedlings and clones** — require 65–75% RH because their underdeveloped root systems cannot pull enough water to compensate for transpiration loss.
- **Vegetative stage** — 50–65% RH. If your room naturally runs below 45% in winter, a humidifier will help.
- **Dry climates** — desert or high-altitude environments where ambient humidity regularly drops below 30%.
- **Winter indoor grows** — heated indoor air in winter can drop to 15–25% RH, which stresses plants and slows growth.

**You likely do not need a humidifier** if your ambient humidity is already above 45% during the vegetative stage or if you are in the flowering stage (where you want humidity low).

### Types of Humidifiers

| Type | How It Works | Pros | Cons | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Evaporative (wick-based)** | Fan blows air through a wet wick/filter | Self-regulating, no mineral dust, inexpensive filters | Requires regular wick replacement, some fan noise | **Recommended** — best overall choice for grow spaces |
| **Evaporative (no wick / natural)** | Large water surface area, passive evaporation | Silent, no electricity, very cheap | Slow output, large footprint | Good for small tents as a passive option |
| **Ultrasonic** | High-frequency vibration creates cool mist | Very quiet, high output, visible mist | Releases mineral dust (white powder) into the air that coats leaves and equipment, requires distilled water | **Avoid** — mineral dust is harmful to plants and equipment |
| **Warm mist (vaporizer)** | Heats water to create steam | Kills bacteria in water, visible output | Uses significant electricity, adds heat (counterproductive in grow spaces), burn hazard | **Avoid** — the heat output works against your temperature goals |

### Humidifier Sizing

Humidifier output is rated in **gallons per day** (or pints per day). Size based on your grow space volume and the humidity deficit:

| Grow Space | Dry Climate (<30% ambient) | Moderate Climate (30–50%) |
|---|---|---|
| 2×4 ft tent | 0.5–1 gallon/day | 0.25–0.5 gallon/day |
| 4×4 ft tent | 1–2 gallons/day | 0.5–1 gallon/day |
| 5×5 ft tent | 2–3 gallons/day | 1–2 gallons/day |
| 8×8 ft room | 4–6 gallons/day | 2–4 gallons/day |

> **Pro tip:** Place your humidifier near the intake of your oscillating fan so the moisture gets distributed throughout the space. Never place it directly under plants — dripping water on the canopy invites disease. Use a humidistat (built-in or external) to automatically turn the humidifier on and off at your target RH.

## Dehumidifiers

Dehumidifiers are arguably the most critical piece of environmental equipment for successful flowering. High humidity during bloom is the #1 cause of bud rot, which can destroy an entire crop in days.

### Why Dehumidification Is Critical

- **Botrytis (bud rot) prevention** — Botrytis cinerea thrives at RH above 55%. Dense cannabis colas trap moisture inside, creating the perfect environment for this fungus. Keeping RH at 40–50% during flowering is the single most effective botrytis prevention strategy.
- **VPD management** — as temperature increases (from lights), the air's capacity to hold water increases. A dehumidifier keeps absolute humidity in check so VPD stays in the optimal 1.0–1.5 kPa range during flower.
- **Prevents mold and mildew** — walls, ducting, and equipment in humid grow spaces are susceptible to mold growth that can spread to plants.
- **Drying and curing** — the same dehumidifier is used in your drying room to bring harvested buds down to the correct moisture content.

### Types of Dehumidifiers

| Type | How It Works | Pros | Cons | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Compressor (refrigerant)** | Cools air below dew point, condenses water on cold coils | Most common, energy-efficient at warm temps, high capacity | Efficiency drops significantly below 65°F, generates some heat | Standard grow rooms, flowering stage, warm environments |
| **Desiccant** | Moisture-absorbing material (silica gel or similar) rotates between air streams | Works well at low temperatures (down to 34°F), quieter, lighter | Uses more electricity, lower capacity, desiccant wheel needs eventual replacement | Cold grow spaces, basements in winter, seedling rooms |
| **Built-in pump** | Compressor model with integrated condensate pump | Can drain upward to a sink or drain, no bucket to empty | More expensive, pump can fail | Permanent setups where gravity drain is not available |
| **Manual drain (bucket)** | Collects water in an internal reservoir | Cheapest option | Must be emptied 1–3× daily in a grow space, will stop working if full | Small tents, temporary setups |

### Dehumidifier Sizing Table

Dehumidifier capacity is rated in **pints of water removed per 24 hours**. These ratings are based on standard testing conditions (80°F, 60% RH). Real-world performance in a grow space will be lower.

| Grow Space | Flowering Stage (target 40–50% RH) | Vegetative Stage (target 50–65%) |
|---|---|---|
| 2×4 ft tent | 20–30 pints/day | 10–20 pints/day |
| 4×4 ft tent | 30–50 pints/day | 20–30 pints/day |
| 5×5 ft tent | 50–70 pints/day | 30–50 pints/day |
| 8×8 ft room | 70–100+ pints/day | 50–70 pints/day |
| 10×10 ft room | 100–150+ pints/day | 70–100 pints/day |

> **Important:** Always oversize your dehumidifier. A unit rated for 50 pints/day will likely remove closer to 30–35 pints/day in real grow tent conditions. If you are on the fence between two sizes, buy the larger one. Running a larger dehumidifier at a lower setting is more efficient and gives you capacity for the peak humidity days that come with heavy transpiration during lights-on.

### Dehumidifier Placement and Drainage

- **Placement** — position the dehumidifier where air can flow freely into and out of it. Do not push it flush against a wall. In a tent, this usually means outside the tent with a return duct, or in the room the tent is located.
- **Continuous drain** — highly recommended. Set up a hose to a floor drain or utility sink. Emptying a bucket multiple times per day during flowering is tedious, and if the bucket fills up, the dehumidifier stops and humidity spikes.
- **Outside the tent** — for small tents, the best practice is to place the dehumidifier in the room that contains the tent, and control the room's humidity. The tent's inline fan will pull the conditioned air from the room through the intake.

## Temperature Control

Temperature management works alongside humidity management — the two are inseparably linked through VPD. Target temperature ranges for cannabis:

| Stage | Temperature (Lights On) | Temperature (Lights Off) |
|---|---|---|
| Seedling/Clone | 72–78°F (22–26°C) | 68–72°F (20–22°C) |
| Vegetative | 70–82°F (21–28°C) | 65–70°F (18–21°C) |
| Flowering (early-mid) | 68–78°F (20–26°C) | 62–68°F (17–20°C) |
| Flowering (late / flush) | 65–75°F (18–24°C) | 60–65°F (15–18°C) |

### Portable AC Units

When ventilation alone cannot keep temperatures in range — typically when ambient room temperature exceeds your target — a portable air conditioner becomes necessary.

#### BTU Calculation

Air conditioners are sized in BTUs (British Thermal Units). The correct method for a grow space:

```
Base BTU = Floor Area (sq ft) × 20-30 BTU/sq ft
Light BTU = Actual Watt Draw × 3.41 BTU/hr per watt
Total BTU = Base BTU + Light BTU + 10-20% for equipment heat and ambient conditions
```

**Example: 4×4 LED grow (400W actual draw) in a room at 75°F ambient:**
- Base: 16 sq ft × 25 = 400 BTU/hr
- Light: 400W × 3.41 = 1,364 BTU/hr
- Total: 400 + 1,364 + 10% ≈ 2,000 BTU/hr minimum
- Real-world recommendation: 3,000-5,000 BTU AC (oversize for peak summer heat)

**Example: 4×4 HPS grow (600W actual draw):**
- Base: 16 sq ft × 25 = 400 BTU/hr
- Light: 600W × 3.41 = 2,046 BTU/hr
- Total: 400 + 2,046 + 15% ≈ 2,800 BTU/hr minimum
- Real-world recommendation: 5,000-8,000 BTU AC

| Grow Space | LED Light Estimate | HPS Light Estimate |
|---|---|---|
| 4×4 ft tent | 3,000–5,000 BTU | 5,000–8,000 BTU |
| 5×5 ft tent | 4,000–6,000 BTU | 8,000–10,000 BTU |
| 8×8 ft room | 8,000–12,000 BTU | 12,000–18,000 BTU |

#### Single-Hose vs Dual-Hose

| | Single-Hose | Dual-Hose |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Pulls air from the room, cools it, exhausts hot air outside through one hose | Pulls outside air for the condenser, exhausts it back outside, cools room air separately |
| Effect on room pressure | Creates negative pressure in the room, pulling in unconditioned air from elsewhere | Neutral pressure |
| Efficiency | Less efficient — the room it's cooling is the same room it's pulling air from | More efficient — dedicated intake and exhaust |
| Cost | Cheaper | More expensive |
| Recommendation | Acceptable for small spaces if the room is well-sealed | Preferred for dedicated grow rooms |

**Important consideration:** A portable AC unit exhausts hot air. In a sealed grow room, you need to plan where that hot air goes — through a window kit, into an adjacent room, or through dedicated exhaust. If the hot air exhausts back into the same sealed room, the AC will work against itself.

#### AC Placement Tips

- The hot air exhaust hose must vent to a space that does not recirculate back into the grow area.
- Keep the exhaust hose as short as possible and insulated — the hose itself radiates heat.
- A dual-hose unit is strongly preferred for dedicated grow rooms because it does not create negative pressure that fights your ventilation system.

### Evaporative Coolers (Swamp Coolers)

Evaporative coolers work by passing warm air through wet pads, cooling the air through evaporation. They use a fraction of the electricity of a compressor AC.

**They work well when:**
- Ambient humidity is low (below 40% RH)
- Ambient temperature is high (above 85°F / 29°C)
- You have good ventilation to exhaust the added humidity

**They do NOT work when:**
- Ambient humidity is already above 50% RH — the cooling effect drops dramatically
- You are in the flowering stage and need to keep humidity low — adding moisture is counterproductive
- You are in a humid climate — they provide minimal cooling and make humidity problems worse

Swamp coolers are primarily useful for **vegetative stage grows in hot, dry climates** (desert Southwest, Mediterranean summers). For flowering or humid climates, stick with ventilation and compressor AC.

## Environmental Controllers

An environmental controller is the brain of your grow space. It reads sensors and automatically turns equipment on and off (or adjusts speed) to keep your environment in the target ranges. Without one, you are manually adjusting fans and checking hygrometers — which is imprecise and labor-intensive.

### What Controllers Automate

- **Exhaust fan speed** — increase when temperature exceeds threshold, decrease when it drops.
- **Humidifier** — turn on when RH drops below target, off when it reaches the upper threshold.
- **Dehumidifier** — turn on when RH exceeds the upper target (typically via a smart plug or built-in relay).
- **Oscillating fans** — set schedules to run during lights-on only (or 24/7 if preferred).
- **Lights** — on/off timer for your photoperiod. Some controllers also support light intensity dimming.
- **CO₂ supplementation** — turn on CO₂ when levels drop below target and lights are on.
- **Temperature alarms** — alert you via app or text when conditions go outside safe ranges.

### Types of Controllers

| Type | Capabilities | Best For | Price Range |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple thermostat/hygrostat** | Single outlet, triggers on one parameter (temp OR humidity) | Basic setups, single-device control | $15–$30 |
| **Multi-outlet analog** | 2–4 outlets, each with its own sensor trigger (temp, humidity, timer) | Hobby tents, moderate automation | $40–$80 |
| **Digital multi-zone** | 6–8+ outlets, programmable schedules, multiple sensor inputs, min/max logging | Serious hobbyists, multi-tent setups | $80–$200 |
| **WiFi/smart controllers** | All of the above + app control, remote monitoring, alerts, data logging, IFTTT integration | Tech-forward growers, remote monitoring | $100–$300 |
| **Integrated systems** | Proprietary ecosystem with matching fans, sensors, and app. Everything works together out of the box. | Growers who want a seamless, single-brand solution | $200–$500+ |

### Controller Brand Tiers

| Tier | Brands | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Budget** | Inkbird, Generic digital thermostats | Basic on/off control, single sensor, no scheduling. Gets the job done for simple setups. |
| **Mid** | AC Infinity Controller 67/69, Titan Controls, Thermopro | Multi-outlet, multiple sensor types, scheduling, min/max tracking, some with app connectivity. |
| **Premium** | AC Infinity Controller 67 Pro/69 Pro, TrolMaster Hydro-X, Autopilot | Multi-zone control, advanced programming, data logging, smartphone app, sensor fusion, expandable relay boxes. |

### Controller Setup Recommendations

**For a basic tent setup**, you need:
- Temperature-triggered exhaust fan control
- Humidity-triggered humidifier/dehumidifier control
- Timer for lights

**For a serious setup**, add:
- VPD-based control (using combined temperature and humidity)
- Remote alerts (phone notification if temperature goes too high or too low)
- CO₂ controller integration
- Data logging to review trends and identify problems before they become crises

### Sensor Placement

Where you place your environmental sensors matters enormously:

- **Height** — place the sensor at **canopy level**, not at the top of the tent or on the floor. The canopy is where the plant actually experiences the environment.
- **Airflow** — do not place the sensor directly in front of a fan or the inline exhaust duct. You want an average reading, not an extreme.
- **Light shielding** — if your sensor reads temperature, shield it from direct light. Radiant heat from grow lights will give falsely high readings.
- **Multiple sensors** — in large rooms, use sensors at multiple locations and control based on the average or the worst-case reading.

## Ducting and Accessories

Ducting connects your inline fan, carbon filter, and exhaust point. The right ducting minimizes airflow resistance, prevents light leaks, and keeps your setup clean.

### Ducting Types

| Type | Description | Pros | Cons | Best For |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Flexible aluminum** | Single-layer aluminum foil on a wire helix | Cheap, lightweight, flexible, easy to install | Noisy (amplifies fan vibration), prone to tearing, no insulation | Short runs, budget builds |
| **Insulated flex duct** | Aluminum inner layer, foam insulation, PVC outer jacket | Quieter, prevents condensation, maintains air temperature | More expensive, bulkier, harder to route | Long runs, runs through cold/hot spaces |
| **Rigid PVC/ABS pipe** | Solid plastic pipe | Smoothest interior (lowest resistance), very quiet, durable | Expensive, requires fittings for turns, permanent | Permanent room builds |
| **Fabric ducting (soxx)** | Permeable fabric tube that diffuses air along its length | Even air distribution, very quiet, looks clean | Not for intake (only for distributing conditioned air), specialized | Air distribution in large rooms |

### Ducting Sizing

**Always match your ducting diameter to your fan and filter flange size.** Using a reducer (e.g., 6" fan to 4" duct) creates a bottleneck that dramatically increases noise and reduces CFM.

| Fan/Filter Size | Minimum Duct Size | Recommended Duct Size |
|---|---|---|
| 4" | 4" | 4" |
| 6" | 6" | 6" (or 8" with reducer at fan for quieter operation) |
| 8" | 8" | 8" |

> **Pro tip:** If your fan supports it, step up the ducting size right after the fan (e.g., 6" fan to 8" duct, then back to 6" at the exhaust). Larger duct = slower air velocity = less noise and less resistance. This is called "oversizing" and is a common technique in quiet grow builds.

### Essential Ducting Accessories

- **Duct clamps** — use metal ratchet clamps or heavy-duty worm-drive clamps, not the thin aluminum twist-ties that come with cheap ducting. Proper clamps prevent air leaks that kill your negative pressure.
- **Light traps / light hoods** — baffled intake covers that allow air to flow but block all light paths. Essential for passive intake ports on tents. Without them, light leaks in through the intake.
- **Y-splitters** — split one duct run into two (e.g., one fan cooling two tents, or one intake serving two zones). Each split reduces airflow to each branch by roughly 30–40%.
- **Backdraft damper** — a one-way flap that prevents air from flowing backward through your exhaust when the fan is off. Keeps cold/hot outside air from entering and prevents odor escape when the fan is off.
- **Duct hangers / supports** — do not let the fan's flange bear the full weight of long duct runs. Support the duct to reduce vibration transmission.

## Negative Pressure Setup

Negative pressure means the air pressure inside your grow space is slightly **lower** than the pressure outside. This is achieved by having your exhaust fan remove air slightly faster than your intake can replace it.

### Why Negative Pressure Matters

- **Odor containment** — any crack, zipper gap, or port hole will have air flowing **in**, not out. This means unfiltered, odorous air cannot escape. This is your primary defense against smell complaints.
- **Heat containment** — hot air is forced out through the exhaust path only, not leaking through random gaps.
- **Pest barrier** — a slight inward air current makes it harder for flying pests (fungus gnats, thrips) to find their way in through small openings.

### How to Create Negative Pressure

1. **Seal all passive intents** except the one(s) you intend to use. Close all ports, zip all zippers fully.
2. **Set your exhaust fan** to a constant speed that is slightly higher than what your passive intake can match.
3. **Verify** — the walls of your grow tent should bow inward slightly. When you hold your hand near a zipper or seam, you should feel air being pulled in, not pushed out.

### How Much Negative Pressure?

You want **slight** negative pressure — enough that the tent walls bow in by about 0.25–0.5 inches. Excessive negative pressure:
- Makes zippers hard to open
- Can collapse flexible ducting
- Forces your fan to work harder, reducing its effective CFM
- Creates gaps at zipper seams as the tent fabric is pulled tight

### Troubleshooting Negative Pressure

| Problem | Cause | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Tent walls bulging outward | Positive pressure — exhaust is too weak or intake is too large | Increase exhaust fan speed, reduce intake opening |
| No bow at all | Balanced pressure — may be okay but not ideal for odor control | Slightly increase exhaust speed |
| Tent walls collapsing inward | Excessive negative pressure | Increase passive intake opening, reduce exhaust speed |
| Odor still escaping | Exhaust not running continuously, or carbon filter is saturated | Run exhaust 24/7, replace carbon filter |
| Zipper gaps appearing | Too much negative pressure pulling fabric taut | Reduce exhaust speed, increase intake area |

## Light-Proofing Your Setup

Cannabis is a photoperiod-sensitive plant. During the flowering stage, it requires **uninterrupted darkness** for 10–12 hours per day (depending on genetics). Even small light leaks during the dark period can cause stress that leads to hermaphroditism — where female plants develop male pollen sacs, self-pollinate, and produce seeded buds.

### Why Light Leaks Cause Hermaphroditism

Cannabis uses its phytochrome system to measure night length. Even brief exposure to light (as little as 10–15 minutes) during the dark period can reset the plant's internal flowering clock. Repeated disruptions cause stress, and stressed female cannabis plants respond by producing male flowers as a survival mechanism — ensuring they can reproduce before they die. Once a plant herms, the entire crop can become seeded, dramatically reducing flower quality and yield.

### Common Light Leak Points

- **Tent zippers** — the most common leak point. Where two zipper tracks meet, tiny gaps form. Light shines through like a beacon.
- **Ducting ports** — the sleeve where your intake or exhaust ducting passes through the tent wall. If not sealed properly, light enters through the duct.
- **Electrical cord ports** — the small openings where power cables enter the tent.
- **Window seams** — clear or translucent viewing windows on tent doors. These are often not fully light-proof.
- **Stitch holes** — needle holes from the tent's construction can let through pinpricks of light in a dark room.
- **Floor gaps** — where the tent floor meets the walls, especially at corners.
- **Peep holes / access ports** — any port designed for hand access or monitoring.

### How to Light-Proof Intake and Exhaust

**Ducting ports:**
- Use **light trap baffles** — S-shaped or L-shaped duct adapters that allow air to flow but block all direct light paths.
- If running bare ducting through a port, wrap the duct in **blackout material** or use **insulated ducting** (the outer PVC layer blocks light).
- Seal around the duct with **black electrical tape** or **aluminum tape** where it enters the port.

**Passive intake vents:**
- Install **light-proof louvers** — angled baffles that let air in but block light. These can be purchased or 3D-printed.
- Build a **light baffle box** — a simple box with internal partitions that creates a light maze. Air flows through, light cannot.

### General Light-Proofing Techniques

- **Zipper covers** — many tents come with storm flaps over the door zipper. If yours does not, add a strip of black Mylar or heavy fabric over the zipper seam.
- **Black tape** — keep a roll of black electrical tape or gaffer tape handy for sealing any unexpected light leaks you discover during dark period checks.
- **The hand test** — during the dark period, wait 10 minutes for your eyes to adjust, then inspect every seam, zipper, and port from the inside. Any light you see needs to be sealed.
- **Phone camera test** — use your phone camera in a dark tent. Phone cameras are more sensitive to small amounts of light than the human eye and can reveal leaks you might otherwise miss.
- **Avoid white or reflective tape** near seams — use black tape only. Reflective tape can bounce light around inside the tent during light leaks.
- **Check after every maintenance session** — moving equipment, adjusting fans, and working inside the tent can shift fabric and create new gaps. Always verify light-proofing after any significant work inside the space.

### Emergency Light Leak Response

If you discover a light leak during flowering:
1. **Seal it immediately** — use black tape or fabric.
2. **Assess the duration** — if the leak was brief (under 15 minutes) and happened once, plants will likely recover with no issues.
3. **Monitor for stress signs** — watch for unusual leaf positioning, slowed growth, or (worst case) the appearance of pollen sacs / bananas (nanners) in bud sites over the following 1–3 weeks.
4. **Consider Rhoadey stress** — if the leak was significant and prolonged, some growers introduce a 36–48 hour period of continuous darkness to reset the flowering cycle, though this is a last resort and can itself cause stress.
