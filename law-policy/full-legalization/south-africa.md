In September 2018, South Africa's Constitutional Court — the nation's highest court — issued a landmark ruling in the case of *Minister of Justice and Constitutional Development v. Prince* that fundamentally changed the legal status of cannabis in South Africa. The court ruled that the **personal use, possession, and cultivation of cannabis by adults in private is a constitutional right**, protected by the right to privacy enshrined in South Africa's post-apartheid Constitution. The decision made South Africa the first nation in the world to legalize cannabis through a constitutional privacy right — a legal pathway unique to South Africa's rights-based constitutional tradition.

But the ruling left critical questions unanswered. **Commercial sale of cannabis remains illegal.** No regulatory framework for legal sales has been implemented. Adults may possess and cultivate cannabis privately, but they may not buy or sell it legally. This creates a paradoxical situation in which the activity of personal cannabis use is constitutionally protected, but the means of acquiring cannabis (other than growing one's own) remain criminal. The South African government has struggled since the ruling to develop a regulatory framework that respects the Constitutional Court's decision while addressing the commercial, social, and public health dimensions that the court's privacy-based ruling did not resolve.

South Africa's cannabis story is deeply intertwined with the country's colonial and apartheid history. Traditional cannabis (dagga) use has deep roots in indigenous Khoisan and Bantu cultures — cannabis was criminalized under colonial rule and the prohibition was maintained and enforced under apartheid. Post-apartheid cannabis reform is thus not simply a drug policy issue; it is connected to broader conversations about indigenous rights, restorative justice, and the dismantling of colonial-era legal structures that the new Constitution promised to overcome.

The legal battle that led to decriminalization was fought over decades by the "dagga couple" — **Myrtle Clarke and Julian Stobbs** — who became the public faces of South Africa's cannabis reform movement. Julian Stobbs died of cancer in 2017, a year before the Constitutional Court's ruling he had fought for. Myrtle Clarke continued the advocacy, and the court's decision was, in significant part, the fruit of their labor.

## Historical Background

### Indigenous Cannabis Use

Cannabis has been present in southern Africa for centuries, with deep roots in **indigenous Khoisan and Bantu cultures**:

- Cannabis (known as **dagga** in South Africa, from the Khoikhoi word *dacha*) was used by indigenous peoples for medicinal, ritual, and recreational purposes
- The Khoisan peoples used cannabis in traditional medicine and cultural practices
- Bantu-speaking peoples who migrated into southern Africa brought cannabis cultivation and use traditions
- Cannabis was smoked through pipes and used in various preparations
- The plant was integrated into the agricultural and cultural life of pre-colonial southern African societies

This traditional use was disrupted by **European colonialism**. Dutch and later British colonial administrators imposed European moral and legal frameworks on indigenous practices, criminalizing substances that had been used legally for generations.

### Colonial Criminalization

Cannabis was criminalized in South Africa under **colonial rule**:

- The British colonial administration imposed drug laws that criminalized cannabis
- These laws reframed indigenous cultural practices as criminal behavior
- The criminalization was part of the broader colonial project of imposing European legal, moral, and cultural norms on colonized populations
- Enforcement was racially biased, targeting indigenous and Black South African cannabis users while often tolerating white users

### Apartheid-Era Enforcement

Under apartheid (1948-1994), cannabis enforcement was **racially structured**:

| Dynamic | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Racialized enforcement** | Black, Coloured, and Indian South Africans faced disproportionate cannabis enforcement. White South Africans were less likely to be arrested, prosecuted, or imprisoned for cannabis offenses. |
| **Harsh penalties** | The Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act (1992), passed in the final years of apartheid, established severe penalties for cannabis offenses, including mandatory minimum sentences. |
| **Political prisoners** | Some anti-apartheid activists used cannabis, and the criminalization of cannabis was one more tool in the apartheid state's arsenal of social control. |
| **Rastafari persecution** | South Africa's Rastafari communities, which use cannabis as a sacrament, faced harassment, arrest, and prosecution under apartheid drug laws. |

The apartheid-era drug law framework was designed and implemented within a system of institutionalized racial oppression. The laws themselves were facially neutral, but their enforcement was structured by the racial hierarchies of apartheid society.

### Post-Apartheid Continuity

After the end of apartheid in 1994, South Africa adopted one of the world's most progressive Constitutions, with an expansive Bill of Rights. However, the **drug law framework was not reformed**:

- The Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act (1992) remained in force
- Cannabis remained criminalized
- Enforcement continued, still with racial disparities
- The new democratic government had many urgent priorities (housing, healthcare, education, economic transformation), and drug law reform was not at the top of the agenda

The continuity of cannabis prohibition in post-apartheid South Africa is an example of how colonial and apartheid-era legal structures persisted in the new democracy, awaiting challenge through the constitutional framework that the apartheid state could never have imagined.

### The Dagga Couple

![Myrtle Clarke and Julian Stobbs — the dagga couple](/uploads/images/law-policy/dagga-couple.jpg)

The legal challenge that ultimately dismantled cannabis prohibition was brought by **Myrtle Clarke and Julian Stobbs**, known in South Africa as the "dagga couple":

- Julian Stobbs (a British-born South African attorney) and Myrtle Clarke (a South African activist) were arrested for cannabis possession and cultivation in 2010
- Rather than accepting penalties, they mounted a constitutional challenge, arguing that cannabis prohibition violated the right to privacy, the right to dignity, and the right to freedom of conscience
- Their case worked its way through the court system over several years
- Julian Stobbs was diagnosed with cancer and died in 2017, before the Constitutional Court's ruling
- Myrtle Clarke continued the fight, and the Constitutional Court's 2018 decision was the culmination of their legal strategy and personal sacrifice

The dagga couple's case is one of the most significant public interest litigation efforts in South African legal history. Their persistence over nearly a decade, through personal tragedy and legal setback, ultimately changed the constitutional landscape of cannabis in South Africa.

### The Prince Case

The Constitutional Court case — *Minister of Justice and Constitutional Development v. Prince* (CCT 320/17) — was decided on **September 18, 2018**:

- The court ruled, unanimously, that adults have a constitutional right to use, possess, and cultivate cannabis **in private** for personal consumption
- The right is grounded in the **right to privacy** (Section 14 of the Constitution) and the **right to dignity** (Section 10)
- The court declared that the provisions of the Drugs and Drug Trafficking Act and the Medicines and Related Substances Control Act that criminalized these activities were unconstitutional to the extent that they prohibited private personal use
- The court suspended the declaration of invalidity for 24 months to allow Parliament to correct the defects (this deadline was extended)

Critically, the court's ruling was **limited to private personal use**:

- It did not legalize commercial sale
- It did not legalize public possession
- It did not legalize distribution
- It did not create a regulatory framework

The court's reasoning was specifically about the constitutional right of adults to make personal decisions about what they consume in the privacy of their own homes — not about cannabis policy more broadly. This left the South African government with the task of building a regulatory framework around a constitutional right that it had not created and might not have chosen.

## How the System Works (Or Does Not)

### The Constitutional Right

The Constitutional Court's ruling established a **constitutional right** to:

- **Use** cannabis as an adult
- **Possess** cannabis for personal use
- **Cultivate** cannabis plants for personal consumption
- All **in private**

The term "in private" is critical: the right does not extend to public spaces, public consumption, or any form of commercial activity. What constitutes "private" has not been precisely defined by the courts, creating uncertainty.

### The Regulatory Gap

The South African government has **not implemented a comprehensive regulatory framework** for personal cannabis:

- No legislation has been passed to define possession limits, cultivation limits, or the parameters of "private" use
- No regulatory body has been established for personal cannabis oversight
- No quality control, testing, or labeling standards exist for personal cannabis
- No licensing system for any form of cannabis commerce has been created

The proposed **Cannabis for Personal Purposes Bill** would address many of these gaps:

- Establishing quantitative limits for possession and cultivation
- Defining "private" use more precisely
- Creating a framework for cannabis social clubs
- Addressing transportation of cannabis
- Establishing enforcement guidelines

But as of early 2026, this bill has not been enacted.

### Medical Cannabis

South Africa's **Cannabis for Medical Purposes Regulations (2020)** created a functional medical cannabis framework:

- SAHPRA (South African Health Products Regulatory Authority) oversees medical cannabis
- Patients may access cannabis through prescription
- Medical cannabis may be imported or domestically produced under license
- Several South African companies have obtained licenses for medical cannabis production
- South Africa has positioned itself as a potential medical cannabis exporter

The medical framework is the most developed part of South Africa's cannabis regulatory landscape, but it is accessible only to patients with prescriptions and is subject to the cost and bureaucratic barriers typical of medical cannabis programs globally.

### The Commerce Problem

The most significant unresolved issue is **commercial sale**:

- The Constitutional Court protected personal use but did not address commerce
- Adults may grow their own cannabis, but may not buy or sell it
- This creates a practical problem: not everyone can cultivate (lack of space, knowledge, resources)
- It also creates a legal absurdity: one may legally consume cannabis but may not legally acquire it (except through self-cultivation)
- Cannabis gifts between adults exist in a gray area — generally tolerated but not explicitly legal

This gap is the central challenge facing South African cannabis policy. Without a legal supply chain, the constitutional right to personal cannabis use is practically meaningful only for those who can cultivate their own supply.

## Social Justice and Equity

### Indigenous Rights and Restorative Justice

South Africa's cannabis reform is deeply connected to **indigenous rights and restorative justice**:

| Aspect | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Indigenous cannabis traditions** | The Khoisan and Bantu peoples used cannabis for centuries before colonial criminalization. Cannabis reform is connected to the broader project of restoring indigenous cultural practices that were criminalized under colonial rule. |
| **Colonial inheritance** | Cannabis prohibition was imposed by colonial administrators and maintained under apartheid. Dismantling it is part of the broader project of dismantling colonial-era legal structures. |
| **Racial disparities** | Cannabis enforcement under apartheid and in the post-apartheid era has fallen disproportionately on Black South Africans, Coloured communities, and other marginalized groups. |
| **Restorative justice** | Advocates argue that cannabis reform should include reparations for communities most harmed by prohibition, similar to social equity frameworks in the United States. |

### Rastafari Sacramental Use

South Africa's **Rastafari communities** have fought for the right to use cannabis as a sacrament:

- Rastafari religious practice includes the sacramental use of cannabis (ganja)
- Rastafari practitioners have been arrested and prosecuted for cannabis possession under South African law
- Some Rastafari have sought constitutional protection for sacramental cannabis use on religious freedom grounds (Section 15 of the Constitution)
- Court decisions have provided partial protection, but the scope of sacramental cannabis rights remains contested
- The Rastafari community has been an important voice in South Africa's cannabis reform movement, connecting the struggle to both religious freedom and anti-prohibition advocacy

### Social Equity in the Proposed Framework

The proposed Cannabis for Personal Purposes Bill has been criticized for **inadequate social equity provisions**:

- No dedicated social equity licensing framework has been proposed
- No community reinvestment fund from cannabis-related revenue
- No automatic expungement program for prior cannabis convictions
- The bill focuses primarily on defining the parameters of personal use rather than addressing the broader justice dimensions of cannabis reform

Advocates have called for:

- Automatic expungement of prior cannabis convictions
- Social equity licensing when commercial frameworks are established
- Community reinvestment programs funded by cannabis regulation
- Prioritization of indigenous and traditional cannabis farming communities in any future legal market
- Full recognition of Rastafari sacramental cannabis rights

Public opinion in South Africa has shown **growing support for cannabis reform**:

### Political Landscape

| Party/Institution | Position |
|-------------------|----------|
| **ANC (African National Congress)** | Mixed; government has been slow to implement regulatory framework; some members support full legalization, others are cautious |
| **DA (Democratic Alliance)** | Generally supportive of regulated cannabis framework; economic opportunity and individual liberty framing |
| **EFF (Economic Freedom Fighters)** | Supportive of reform; social justice and economic empowerment framing |
| **Constitutional Court** | Pro-reform on privacy grounds; the Prince case ruling was unanimous |
| **SAHPRA** | Focused on medical cannabis regulation; has been functional and professional |
| **Civil society (Fields of Green for All, Dagga Couple, etc.)** | Strong advocacy for full legalization with social justice provisions |

### Government Delay

The South African government has been **criticized for delay** in implementing the regulatory framework required by the Constitutional Court:

- The original 24-month deadline for legislative correction was extended
- Multiple draft bills have been circulated but not enacted
- Advocates have accused the government of dragging its feet on cannabis reform
- The delay has created ongoing legal uncertainty for cannabis consumers and would-be businesses

## Challenges and Criticisms

### The Commerce Gap

The most fundamental challenge is the **absence of legal commerce**:

- Adults have a constitutional right to cannabis but no legal means of acquisition (other than self-cultivation)
- This creates a market gap that is filled by illegal supply
- The illegal supply chain is unregulated, untaxed, and untested for safety
- Would-be legal cannabis businesses cannot operate

### Enforcement Uncertainty

The gap between constitutional law and statutory law creates **enforcement uncertainty**:

- Police officers on the ground may not be fully aware of or trained on the Constitutional Court's ruling
- Cannabis arrests continue, though they should not for activities protected by the court's ruling
- Individuals may be arrested and must then assert their constitutional rights, which requires legal knowledge and resources
- The burden of proof and the definition of "private" remain unclear

### Regulatory Capacity

South Africa's regulatory institutions have **limited capacity** for cannabis oversight:

- SAHPRA has focused on medical cannabis and has been effective in that domain
- No dedicated body has been established for personal cannabis regulation
- Building regulatory capacity requires resources that the South African government has many competing demands for

### Economic Opportunity Lost

South Africa has the potential to be a **significant cannabis producer and exporter**:

- Favorable climate for cannabis cultivation
- Existing agricultural infrastructure
- Medical cannabis export potential
- Domestic market of 60+ million people

But the unresolved legal framework has prevented this potential from being realized. Other African nations (Lesotho, Zimbabwe) have moved more quickly on medical cannabis licensing, and South Africa risks losing its competitive position.

## International Context

### African Leadership

South Africa's Constitutional Court ruling has been **influential across Africa**:

- It demonstrated that cannabis reform can be achieved through constitutional rights arguments, not just legislative action
- It influenced reform debates in other African nations
- Lesotho became the first African nation to grant medical cannabis cultivation licenses (2017), predating South Africa's ruling
- Zimbabwe, Malawi, Zambia, and Morocco have all expanded medical cannabis or hemp programs
- South Africa's rights-based approach offers a different pathway from the legislative approaches seen in other nations

### UN Treaty Compliance

South Africa's personal cannabis framework is in tension with the **1961 UN Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs**, but the tension is mitigated by:

- The absence of commercial sale (the treaty is primarily concerned with production and supply chains)
- The personal, private nature of the right (consistent with a narrow interpretation of the treaty's medical and scientific purpose exception)
- South Africa's continued participation in the international drug control system through its medical cannabis program

### Rastafari International

The South African Rastafari community's fight for sacramental cannabis use connects to a **global Rastafari rights movement**:

- Rastafari communities in Jamaica, the United States, the United Kingdom, and other nations have fought for sacramental cannabis rights
- South Africa's constitutional framework provides a potentially powerful model for Rastafari advocates in other jurisdictions
- The intersection of religious freedom and cannabis rights is an underexplored area of international human rights law

## Key Takeaways

- **South Africa's Constitutional Court ruled in 2018** (Prince case) that personal use, possession, and cultivation of cannabis in private is a constitutional right protected by the right to privacy — the first nation to legalize cannabis through a privacy right.
- **Commercial sale remains illegal** — no regulatory framework for legal cannabis commerce has been implemented. Adults may possess and grow cannabis privately but may not buy or sell it.
- **The "dagga couple" (Myrtle Clarke and Julian Stobbs)** fought the legal battle that led to the Constitutional Court's ruling over nearly a decade. Julian Stobbs died of cancer in 2017, before the decision.
- **Indigenous cannabis traditions** (Khoisan and Bantu) predate colonial criminalization. Cannabis reform is connected to broader indigenous rights and restorative justice conversations in post-apartheid South Africa.
- **Rastafari communities** have fought for sacramental cannabis use rights, achieving partial success through the constitutional framework.
- **Medical cannabis** is regulated through SAHPRA (2020 regulations), creating a functional medical pathway while recreational commerce remains unresolved.
- **Enforcement uncertainty persists** — the gap between constitutional law and statutory law means cannabis consumers and cultivators face ongoing legal risk despite the Constitutional Court's ruling.
- **Economic opportunity is unrealized** — South Africa's favorable climate and agricultural capacity position it as a potential major cannabis producer and exporter, but the unresolved legal framework prevents this potential from being realized.
